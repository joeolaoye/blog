“We have 100,000 users.”

It sounds like success. It looks good in a pitch deck, makes a strong announcement and gives the team something to celebrate.

But it leaves some important questions unanswered.

How many of those users still use the product? How many pay? What does it cost to serve them? And if you stopped spending money to attract more users, would there still be a business?

A user count tells you how many people you have attracted. It does not tell you whether you have built something commercially sustainable.

## A signup is a beginning

People sign up for all sorts of reasons. Curiosity. A free offer. A recommendation. A problem they hope your product will solve.

Some never finish onboarding. Some try the product once. Some create accounts and forget they exist.

Yet all of them can remain in the number a founder quotes when asked how the business is doing.

That is how a useful measurement becomes a vanity metric: the number keeps growing while its connection to business performance gets weaker.

Registered users, active users and paying customers are different things. Treating them as interchangeable makes it easier to feel successful and harder to understand what needs fixing.

## A smaller audience can support a better business

Imagine two software companies.

The first has 100,000 registered users. Of those, 500 pay $10 a month. It generates $5,000 in monthly revenue.

The second has 2,000 registered users. Of those, 400 pay $50 a month. It generates $20,000 in monthly revenue.

The first company has fifty times as many registered users. The second earns four times as much.

That does not automatically make the second company healthier. Its costs could be higher, its customers could leave faster, or its revenue could depend on a few fragile relationships.

But it shows why the headline number tells us so little.

The useful question is what happens after someone arrives.

Do they find value? Do they return? Do they pay? Do they stay long enough for the relationship to make commercial sense?

## Revenue still needs to survive the costs

Even “we make money from our users” is only part of the answer.

Suppose a customer pays you $20 a month, but serving them costs $18 in infrastructure, support and third-party services. That leaves $2 before salaries, marketing and other operating expenses.

If acquiring that customer costs $100, recovering the acquisition cost takes 50 months at that contribution level, assuming they stay and the economics remain unchanged.

You have revenue. You may also have a serious problem.

This matters particularly for products where usage creates substantial costs. An AI product can become more popular while its model bills grow faster than its income. A payments business can process impressive volumes while retaining very little after partner fees, losses and support.

Growth amplifies the economics you already have. If those economics are poor, more users can make the problem larger.

## Free users can have value

None of this means every user must pay directly.

An advertising business earns from advertisers. A marketplace may need participants on both sides before transactions become useful. A free product can help paying teams adopt a wider service.

Free users can contribute distribution, content, referrals or marketplace activity.

But that contribution needs a credible connection to the business.

“Free users are valuable” should lead to a specific explanation: they attract paying customers, improve transaction completion, lower acquisition costs or create something buyers will pay to access.

“We will monetise later” is a hypothesis. It becomes a strategy when you explain who will pay, what they will pay for and how you will test that assumption.

## Measure the journey to a viable business

User count belongs on the dashboard. It just should not dominate it.

Alongside it, track:

- **Activation:** Are new users reaching the point where the product becomes useful?
- **Retention:** Do they keep coming back?
- **Conversion:** Do enough become paying customers?
- **Revenue quality:** Does income recur, grow or disappear?
- **Cost to serve:** What remains after delivering the service?
- **Acquisition payback:** How long does it take to recover what you spent winning a customer?

These measures help explain growth instead of merely announcing it.

They also change your decisions. You might improve onboarding before buying more traffic. Raise prices before expanding features. Focus on a smaller customer segment that gets more value and stays longer.

Those decisions may produce less impressive signup announcements. They can produce a stronger company.

## Build something worth paying for

There is nothing wrong with celebrating users. Getting people to try something you built is an achievement.

The danger is allowing that achievement to stand in for the business itself.

A large audience can create opportunity. Turning that opportunity into a business requires a repeatable way to deliver value, capture some of that value as income and cover the cost of doing so.

The number of users you have is a vanity metric when it is detached from those realities.

What you earn from serving them, what it costs to earn it and whether they keep coming back is where the business begins.
